## Headline finding

**The project's stated differentiator is the meshing hypothesis, and the meshing hypothesis is not merely unsupported — as of Lyle et al. 2023 it is contradicted by a positive finding, not just null results.** "Same canonical content, AI re-shapes it as overview / chat-lesson / quiz based on user profile" is format-matching to a learner profile. That is the exact claim education research has been trying and failing to confirm since 2008, and the one recent study that tested a non-sensory version of it (active/reflective preference vs interactive/non-interactive lesson — structurally the closest published analogue to overview-vs-chat-vs-quiz) found learners did **better when the format MISmatched their preference**.

Adaptation is not dead. But the dimensions that survive contact with the evidence are **prior knowledge**, **difficulty calibration**, and **step-level feedback granularity** — none of which require a user profile in the "learner type" sense, and all of which are 30-year-old ITS commodities rather than a moat.

## Verdict on adaptive rendering per profile

**Not defensible.** Stated flatly:

1. **"Same canonical content, re-shaped as overview / chat-lesson / quiz based on user profile" is the meshing hypothesis** with the modality axis swapped for a format axis. Swapping the axis does not rescue it — Lyle et al. 2023 tested exactly that kind of non-sensory axis (active/reflective preference × interactive vs non-interactive lesson) and found matching *hurt* on the targeted measure.
2. **The EUREKA is backwards.** "They all chose ONE format; we can choose all three per user" reads the competitors' convergence as a limitation. The evidence says convergence on one well-designed format is the *correct* move (claim 19: main effects, not interactions), and that the differentiator's premise inverts a known null into a feature.
3. **"The user model is the moat" has no meta-analytic support.** Claim 11: ITS gains show up regardless of whether the system modelled the student. Claim 10: the effect tracks *interaction granularity*, not profile fidelity. Whatever moat exists is in the inner loop, not the user model.
4. **The supporting literature it would cite is the weakest tier available** (claim 17): n=23, p<.10, preference outcomes. Kestin et al. 2025, already in the repo, does **not** support it — that tutor won on pedagogical scaffolding, not on profile-driven format switching.

One honest qualification: **nobody has run the specific experiment** — a well-powered RCT of LLM-generated overview vs dialogue vs quiz renderings of identical content, assigned by profile vs randomly, with learning outcomes at delay. The verdict above is a strong prior from a large adjacent literature with a consistent direction and one direct-analogue reversal, not a direct refutation of this exact product. That prior is strong enough that building the differentiator on it would be a bet against the field.

**What can still be built:** offering the three renderings as a **user-selectable choice** is defensible as autonomy support (claim 18) and costs nothing pedagogically, provided all three are independently well-designed. What is not defensible is *inferring* the right format from a profile, *claiming* it teaches better, or *calling it the moat*.

## What adaptation IS defensible — ranked by evidence strength

1. **Prior knowledge / expertise reversal (STRONGEST).** Large, replicated, directional: support that helps a novice harms an expert. Adapt scaffolding density, worked-example completeness, and guidance fading to *demonstrated* prior knowledge — measured from performance, never self-reported. [R7][R8]
2. **Step-level interaction granularity (STRONGEST for product shape).** VanLehn's d≈0.76 for step-based vs d≈0.31 for answer-based is the single largest actionable lever found in this sweep, and it is about *how finely the system responds*, not who the user is. [R12]
3. **Difficulty calibration.** Keep success rate in the productive band rather than letting the learner pick. Robust as a principle; treat ~85% as a target, not a promise. [R9]
4. **Context/domain personalisation (MODERATE — and this is the real differentiator candidate).** Personalising the *scenario domain* to the learner's actual project — their stack, their business, their problem — has genuine support (d≈0.35, up to d≈0.53 for the strugglers) and aligns with the repo's existing "interest > importance" ADHD prior. This is personalisation of **what the example is about**, not **what shape the content takes**. [R15][R16]
5. **Utility-value framing (MODERATE).** Explicit "here's why this matters for what you're building" — better replicated than most ed interventions, largest for low-confidence learners, though with partial-replication caveats. [R17]
6. **Pacing and sequencing (WEAK as personalisation).** System-controlled pacing calibrated to performance is fine; *learner-controlled* pacing is not a learning intervention and learners are poorly calibrated judges of it. [R10][R11]
7. **Format matched to profile (ABSENT / CONTRADICTED).** Do not build. [R1][R2][R3][R4]

**[INFERENCE]** The strategically interesting consequence: items 1–3 are exactly what intelligent tutoring systems have done since the 1990s, so they are table stakes rather than a moat; item 4 — the learner's real project as the scenario source — is the only dimension here that is both evidence-supported and genuinely hard for a generic competitor to copy, because it requires knowing the user's actual work. If a moat is wanted, that is where the evidence points it. This is my reading of the strategic implication, not a finding from any cited paper.

## The honest gap

- **The exact experiment has never been run.** No RCT tests LLM-generated overview vs chat-lesson vs quiz renderings of identical canonical content, profile-assigned vs randomly assigned, with delayed learning outcomes. The verdict is a strong prior, not a direct refutation.
- **"Profile" is doing ambiguous work.** The evidence kills *preference/style* profiles. It supports *prior-knowledge* profiles. A product that says "user profile" without specifying which is borrowing credibility from one to sell the other — and that ambiguity is the single most important thing to resolve before building.
- **No adult-ADHD-specific evidence exists on any of this**, consistent with the repo's existing finding. Everything above is general adult/student learning science.
- **The LLM-personalisation literature is ~2 years old and mostly poor.** Preference and engagement outcomes dominate; learning-outcome RCTs are rare and tiny. This picture could genuinely change by 2027 — but current evidence does not license building on the optimistic reading now.
- **Effect sizes are not additive and mostly come from K-12/undergraduate populations in formal courses**, not self-directed adult founders learning in 3-minute sessions. Transfer to this ICP and this session length is assumed, not demonstrated.
- **Engagement is a real outcome this review does not dismiss.** Choice, personalised context, and interest framing have genuine motivational support (claims 12, 13, 18). For a product whose failure mode is abandonment, motivation may matter more than per-session learning efficiency — but that is a retention argument, and it must be made as one rather than dressed as a pedagogy claim.
